What Makes a Hero?

Children’s Relationships with Tales Across Socio-Economic Contexts in Turkey

Zeynep Canan Vural
Freie Universität Berlin

Originally written for the seminar “Oral Traditions: Remembering/Transmission” (Prof. Khanna Usoyan), 9 December 2025

Note on Methodological Scope

This paper was originally envisioned as an empirical research project, focusing on how children from different socio-economic backgrounds in Turkey relate to narrative materials and construct meaning through stories. The initial design included fieldwork and interviews with children aged 6 to 12. However, due to constraints in time, access, and logistics, the study could not proceed with the planned fieldwork. As a result, it now stands as a theoretical and conceptual groundwork, intended to frame key questions, map relevant literatures, and offer hypotheses that will guide a more comprehensive, field-based master’s thesis in the future.

1. Introduction

Stories, through the long history of humanity, have not only carried the past — they have carried the weight of what we dreamed of, what stayed with us after long episodes of challenges, most human feelings, and the events that transformed the world permanently. Whether transmitted orally, read from books, or shown through the cinema screen, they transformed and are still transforming our present and the future. And for children, stories have a deeper meaning. They're a gateway to their world of imagination and making new meanings of the world they've been living in. That's why when a child or an adult engages with a story, they both experience it differently since children's developmental phase allows possibilities that adults can no longer experience. It's not saying that children have not yet encountered the wildness of the world that is pushed by capitalist structures or that they're raw and innocent, away from the harsh realities of daily lives. The opposite will be argued in this paper: children, like many other marginalized groups, are heavily affected by the inequalities in the environment they live in. What makes children distinct is not the absence of hardship, but their condition of becoming—before identities and ideologies are fully settled, they may still interact with narrative materials from a more instinctive, affective, and open-ended standpoint. Even when forced to grow up too early—through labor, neglect, or hardship—many children remain in touch with their raw emotional vocabulary, engaging with stories in ways that reflect unmet needs, buried fears, or unspoken hopes. Their interpretations might be contradictory, non-linear, or emotionally intense since their language is not shaped by ideology yet. And as scholars such as Jerome Bruner1 (1990) and Jack Zipes2 (2006) have argued, stories are not neutral isolated entities, but they're embedded with values related to gender, class and power. And that's why when we ask children the questions about their favorite tales, we can also expect to get insight about their material, emotional, and cultural conditions of their lives.

This paper will question how children from different socio-economic backgrounds might relate to the stories that they've been told and how they create meanings with them. Focus will be on which stories they might like most, characters they admire, and which values they might find more attractive. And more importantly, what their preferences tell us about the world that these children live in. By questioning their preferences on the stories they love most, this study will seek to understand how material inequality, emotional experience, and symbolic meaning collide. And stories here do not play as a numb actor in the scenario, but they're the active mirrors and maps, both reflecting and shaping children's desires, values, and fears.

This study is situated in a highly specific moment. In 2025, Turkey is experiencing one of the most severe poverties in its recent history3. With the inflation skyrocketing and Turkish lira hitting the bottom, the purchasing power of most of the households has drastically reduced, and a huge proportion of the population is having hardship meeting their basic needs3. According to EUROSTAT data, Turkey ranks first among European countries in terms of the proportion of children at risk of poverty or social exclusion, with an alarming rate of 40%.4 The Turkish Statistical Institute (TÜİK) also adds to this picture with child poverty. Out of every five teenagers aged 15 to 17, one is working.4 And in that same age group, about 320,000 kids can’t get an education at all.4 Also, in the period between 2013-2024 “minimum” 742 child worker has lots their lives.4 A 2024 report5 by Derin Yoksulluk Ağı (Deep Poverty Network) found that 11.8% of children surveyed were working to support their families, and 88% of those working children stated that labor interfered with their education. Children as young as ten contribute their earnings to food or rent. In the first half of 2024 alone, at least 71 children died while working5—a grim reminder that poverty in Turkey is not only material but deadly.

This data reflects the harsh reality in Turkey in the scope of children. And for children under this pressure and precarity, stories are not just entertainment. They're actually symbolic escape rooms where they can experience a sense of freedom apart from their daily struggles and a place where they can also experience their emotions. As cultural theorist Henry Jenkins reminds us, children are not passive consumers of culture; they actively rework narratives through the lens of their own world (Jenkins, p. 1-12)6. Listening to how they engage with tales—what they admire in a character, what “makes sense” to them—reveals how inequality is not only lived materially but also imagined, internalized, or resisted through story. In this research (to be), children are not seen as just interviewees but as narrative agents—interpreters and producers of symbolic meaning—and with their choices, they reflect structural constraints and imaginative possibilities.

The inspiration for this research idea comes directly from the cult book in the field of oral history "Hard Times: An Oral History of the Great Depression" (1970) by Studs Terkel.7 Taking a picture of the American ways of living during the time of the Great Depression, Terkel collects stories of ordinary people navigating economic collapse. This research hasn’t incorporated interviews yet, but it lays the groundwork for something more intimate in the future. The aim is to turn attention to children in Turkey in 2025—to hear their voices firsthand and assemble a more fragmented, truthful picture of how inequality shapes their experiences. Unlike Terkel, who recorded the perspectives of adults already disillusioned by the American Dream, this study focuses on those who haven’t even begun to chase such ambitions. These are children, still learning how to express their emotions and understand the world around them, all while growing up in challenging, unstable conditions.

And this study does not aim to prove the innocence of childhood and celebrating it, yet it is a political commitment to listening to the unheard voices under conditions of deprivation. It asks how tales take place in the lives of children who are coming from different classes, educational opportunities, and cultural capital. Though field interviews were not conducted, the research remains guided by three central questions, which are proposed as the future backbone of a more comprehensive empirical study:

  1. How do children from different socio-economic contexts interpret stories and characters?

  2. What values and emotional attachments surface in their responses?

  3. How do these symbolic preferences reflect the broader structures of class inequality in Turkey?

In answering these questions, the paper contributes to debates in the sociology of childhood, class theory, and cultural meaning-making—placing children not at the margins of society, but at its most telling intersections.

2.1 Childhood as a Classed Experience

The concept of "childhood" today is often understood and used as a utopian space of innocence, an idealized state outside the harsh realities of daily life. Most often, children are showcased as untouched by ideological apparatuses and romanticized as inherently pure, honest or moral. This idealized version of "childhood" is just a recent construct of modernity, and it deliberately positions the children outside of history and class struggle. This modern perspective of childhood, thus, is never universal. Rather, childhood is a concept that histrocially structured and a deeply classed experience.

Historically, childhood as a separate, projected category is a recent invention made after modernity. In premodern, tribal societies, children mostly joined the workforce as soon as they were capable of physical labour, contributed to the production dynamics and were well integrated into the economic and social life of the societies they lived in. With the rise of capitalism, particularly during the Industrial Revoultion in 19th century Britain, the exploitation of human labour also heavily affected the children. Working conditions for children became extremely brutal and children became subjects of exploitation, abuse and deep tragedies as opposed to innocence. But with the rise of modernity, a new understanding of childhood that protects children from the brutal conditions of working life arose and strong steps towards banning the child labour were taken. In this way, childhood, became to put into a separate category that is more sentimental and needs to be protected (Aries, p.128-133). 8

However, this emotional and romantic perspective focused on the innocence of childhood is also utilized a lot in political discourse. Especially right-wing ideologies portray children as the symbolic carriers of the national and moral values into the future by overly highlighting the innocence of childhood (Mikolaj, 86)9. This narrative deliberately seeks to hide that children are deeply embedded within and shaped by the inequalities, conflicts and injustices in the society and isolates them as if they're not involved in this society at all. However, children, just as many marginalized groups, aren't far away from the social reality, but in fact they're the ones who usually get more affected.

Class does not only shapes individuals' material conditions; it also directly penetrates into the cultural and social contexts in which individuals live and deeply influences their values, beliefs and aspirations. This also applies for children. Class differences do not shape children´s lives materially; they also deeply influence the ways in which children make meaning of the world around them. Childhood as a classed experience, therefore, is not only based on the resources that the children might have access to such as food, books, toys or organized activities. More fundamentally, it manifests itself in the meaning-making processes of children—the values they prioritize, the characters they identify with, and the emotional lenses through which they interpret and understand the world.

For instance, even for the widely accessible stories or films like "Little Red Riding Hood," "Cinderella," or contemporary media like "Shrek", children coming from different backgrounds might probably have different perspectives on and create different meanings from the same narratives. A child from a working-class family can admire a character that cleverly navigates hardships, scarcities or dangers and in these characters they can find traces of the struggles of their own lives. On the contrary, a child from middle or upper class family may admire stories or characters with narratives of individual heroism, self-realization or success, reflecting the self-esteem and security they feel in their own lives.

Therefore, class, does not shape only which stories a child can access but also which meanings they may derive from these stories and which values they may glorify. The affective worlds of children—how they experience fear, hope, courage, pride, or shame—are deeply structured by their social positions. These different affective and emotional experiences can easily reflect the emotional aspect of the inequalities in societies.

In contemporary Turkey, the deep division between the children coming from extreme poverty and those raised in wealthy families vividly reveals that childhood is a classed experience. On the one hand, there are children raised in comfort and security, nurtured with cultural capital, and supported to chase their dreams, pursuing their self-realization. And in the other, there are also children whose daily lives are defined by deep despairs such as starvation, child labour, exploitation and suicide. In the next section, the specific case of Turkey will be examined to understand how childhood and class dynamics unfold within the socio-economic context of contemporary Turkish society.

2.2 Socio-Economic Conditions of Childhood in Turkey

In 2025, Turkey is undergoing one of the most acute socio-economic crises in its modern history. While official statistics reported a 3.2% economic growth in 202410, this growth is accompanied by an inflation rate of 44.3% according to the Turkish Statistical Institute (TÜİK)11—and 83.4% according to the independent Inflation Research Group (ENAG)12. Meanwhile, the Turkish Lira has depreciated drastically: the USD/TRY exchange rate, which was 2.71 in 2015, climbed to 38.24 by April 2025—a staggering 1300% devaluation in ten years13.

This macroeconomic collapse has a direct and brutal impact on daily life. In February 2025, the hunger threshold for a family of four was calculated as 23,615 TL, and the poverty threshold as 76,922 TL. Yet the net minimum wage remained at 22,104 TL—meaning that full-time workers still earn below subsistence levels.14 The effects are not merely numerical: they define the texture of everyday life, particularly for children.

Recent socio-economic data underline the structural conditions shaping childhood in Turkey. According to TÜİK and Eurostat, Turkey ranks among the European countries with the highest proportion of children at risk of poverty or social exclusion. Reports indicate that a significant share of children experience limited access to educational resources, digital technologies, and safe leisure spaces, while child labor remains a persistent reality, particularly among lower-income households. These material inequalities directly shape not only children’s daily lives but also the narrative worlds they can access and meaningfully engage with.

According to the 2024 report Yoksulluk Koşullarında Eğitim by Derin Yoksulluk Ağı (Deep Poverty Network), 11.8% of children surveyed reported working to support their families, and 88% of them said that this labor interfered with their education5. The jobs they undertake include waste-picking, flower-selling, shop assistance, and domestic cleaning—often in informal and unsafe environments. In the same year (2024), at least 71 children were reported to have died while working. In between the years 2013 – 2024, this number escalates to 742 total4. These numbers are not isolated tragedies but reflections of systemic exploitation and abandonment.

Beyond direct labor, poverty shapes how childhood is structured. Children growing up in poverty often live in overcrowded homes, lack access to books and toys, and attend severely under-resourced public schools. They may lack private space to read or imagine. Middle- and upper-class children, by contrast, often grow up in homes with their own bedrooms, regular exposure to books and screens, and structured extracurricular activities. These disparities generate entirely different childhoods—affecting not only material well-being, but the development of imagination, language, and narrative thinking.

In this context, asking children about stories is not naïve—it is revealing. A child's admiration for a clever fox, a magical helper, or a brave princess may encode emotional needs or aspirations that reflect their socio-economic world. Some children may resonate with tales of survival or sacrifice; others may be drawn to characters who embody agency, wealth, or escape. In either case, narrative becomes a means through which children articulate and symbolically process inequality.

2.3 Children’s Relationship to Symbolic Material

Why is Meaning-Making Important?

Meaning-making is a fundamental psychological process in which individuals interpret symbols, derive meaning from them, and integrate these interpretations into coherent narratives and symbolic structures. Jerome Bruner, in his famous book "Act of Meaning" (1990), strongly argues that what should be at the center of psychological inquiry is how people interpret and create meanings, and explains how psychological research should focus on understanding how human experience and actions are shaped by "intentional states" such as beliefs, desires and values (Bruner, 34)1. For Bruner, human cognition shouldn´t be reduced to mere computational processing. Rather, he argues, it is an in depth meaning-making mechanism that operates through "intentional states" and this mechanism is deeply connected to the emotional, moral and cultural dimensions. Humans don´t just observe the world, but they actively construct meanings from their culturally available narratives, symbols and stories. These constructed meanings, then, can shape their personal identities, values and affective frameworks, guiding how they interact socially and morally with the world around them.

Another key argument of Bruner1 is that "meaning-making" processes of humans are always occur in cultural frameworks. Culture provides humans a toolbox to interpret the world through symbolic mediums such as language, myths, narratives, images or social roles. This toolbox also enables them to form a self-identity. In childhood, too, these symbolic resources not only construct the world outside them but also create their self. Therefore, children´s world of meaning is not just a personal, isolated space, but in contrast, it´s shaped by cultural structures and social interactions.

Development of Meaning-Making Skills in Childhood: A Developmental Perspective

In order to understand the meaning-making processes of children, it's essential to draw on theories from cognitive developmental psychology. Jean Piaget´s theory of cognitive development and stage theory provides a rich psychological framework explaining the development of symbolic thought and meaning-making abilities through different developmental phases in childhood15. Besides providing a model for stage-based mental maturation, he also sets out a philosophical base for how children move from an individual, egocentric thinking toward a socially constructed, abstract reasoning.

Piaget´s early works first show us that cognitive development is an extension of the biological development of the child. Accordingly, he argues in his initial works that children interact with the world around them through two basic mechanisms: assimilation, in which new information is interpreted through existing cognitive frameworks (schemas), and accommodation, where these frameworks (schemas) are reshaped and adjusted according to the new information. This process, which Piaget termed "equilibration", drives intellectual growth through continuous balancing of internal cognitive structure and external stimuli. For instance, behaviors such as a child's putting objects to their mouth is an example of assimilation of the environment into child´s existing schemas. However, through time, the child gradually begins to realize the existence of the external world and shapes their internal realities accordingly and moves between these two internal and external poles to establish an equilibrium.

In addition to his biological perspective on development, Piaget also introduces a sociological model of development, arguing that children's progression from egocentrism to sociocentrism is possible by the interactions with their peers and adults. With his "semi-clinical interview" method, he analyzed children´s spontenous thoughts to the questions they didn´t expect and realized how children revised their intuitive ideas when exposed to more advanced reasoning by others. This approach shows that the meaning-making process for children is both an individual and a socially mediated activity.

Piaget´s stage theory demonstrates how meaning-making evolves through childhood. In the preoperational stage (~2–7 years), children begin to use symbols and imagination, yet their reasoning is still intuitive and egocentric. They join engage in symbolic play and try to create meanings from the world around them by asking, "why?". However, in this stage, they struggle with logical consistency. In the concrete operational stage (~7–11 years), children develop logical thinking skills and begin to understand cause-and-effect relationships, classification, and perspective-taking. This cognitive leap enables them to interpret the narratives more deeply, make moral assessments and reflect on symbolic meaning. In the formal operational stage (11+), children acquire the abstract thinking capacity that allows them to assess ethical dilemmas, think about hypothetical situations, and interpret the narratives in a more critical and ideologically aware way.

Why Focus on Children Aged 6–12 Years?

This study focuses on children between the ages of 6 and 12 because this developmental stage marks a unique cognitive and symbolic transition. In Piaget’s theory, this age range bridges the shift from intuitive and egocentric meaning-making (preoperational stage) to more logical and socially coordinated narrative reasoning (concrete operational stage). Children at this age begin not only to be able to grasp the structure of the narratives but also their underlying moral, emotional and symbolic content. They become able to compare different values, question the motivations of the characters and recognize patterns through different kinds of narratives. This meta-narrative awareness is especially important for examining the value systems depending on the class. Because this allows researchers to observe how children from different socioeconomic backgrounds interpret the same symbolic material according to their emotional repertoire and cultural capital.

Jerome Bruner (1990) emphasizes that these years in child development are particularly important for establishing narrative as a mode of thought1 (Brumer, 11) . He argues that, during this period, children begin to perceive narratives not merely as sequences of events but as fundamental tools through which they make sense of both the world around them and their own inner selves. They begin to use narratives to justify behaviors, interpret relationships and test emotional or moral possibilities. Katherine Nelson (2007) also argues that in this special period in childhood, the narrative capabilities and moral reasoning develop through social interactions (Nelson ,188) 16. In this age, the interpretation skills of a child are no longer individual; they begin to be shaped by peer relations and cultural norms.

Therefore, 6-12-year-old children represent the desired population for analyzing classed meaning-making processes because they´re cognitively and socially equipped to both receive and reconstruct symbolic narratives—making them highly generative subjects for investigating how stories mediate emotional life, value orientation, and classed perception.

Why Meaning-Making is Deeply Connected to Class?

Children’s meaning-making processes don't occur in a vacuum. Instead, they are influenced by the material conditions, emotional climates, social norms, and symbolic environments that children live in. Class, here, has a significant impact on children's access to different types of narratives, but also it drastically influences how children understand those narratives, including the moral frameworks they internalize, the emotional tones they identify, and the values that speak to them.

Research by scholars such as Annette Lareau (2003) and Barbara Rogoff (1990) demonstrates that children from different socioeconomic backgrounds socialize into different interpretive styles (Lareau, p. 1-32). Middle-class families often practice "concerted cultivation," in which parents actively encourage their children´s reasoning, emotional expression and exposure to structured narratives. These children mostly learn to interact with stories as exercises in self-exploration and personal development. On the contrary, children from working-class families or children that live in precarity may have fewer structured narrative encounters, but this does not mean that their meaning-making process is less rich. As Lareau argues, these children´s meaning-making skills grow with shared routes, affective resonance and collective experiences. This difference between these two different child-rearing forms reveals that class not only shapes children´s relationship with stories in the sense of accessing the material or which material they´re exposed to but also shows that class deeply structures the way children interpret the narratives that they encounter. A fairy tale, a fable or even a character like Shrek may hold quite distinct meanings depending on the child´s lived reality, affective landscape and cultural capital.

Even when we covered extensively on Piaget´s stage theory for its valuable contribution to understanding children´s cognitive development through their ages, from a class perspective, Piaget´s theory is also criticized for assuming a universal line for development for children having different socioeconomic backgrounds. Scholars such as Lareau and Bruner point out to us that cognitive development and meaning-making processes can not be considered separate from class structures. Lareu, in her work, demonstrates that abstract thinking and symbolic interpretation are constructed unevenly among different class lines in childhood (Lareau, 1-11)17. Bruner (1990), similarly, stresses that meaning is always constructed through cultural and social structures1. Therefore, when analyzing how children interpret stories, it´s necessary to consider not only the cognitive processes but also the socio-material conditions in which these processes occur.

In conclusion, meaning-making is not only a developmental milestone for children, but it´s rather a classed, affective and symbolic act that both reflects and reproduces the structural inequalities that children encounter in their daily lives.

2.4 Narratives as Emotional and Cultural Carriers in Childhood

Storytelling is one of the oldest practices of humankind—older than writing, religions, or even agriculture. Long before the history has recorded them, the stories had been told around the fires, pictured on the walls of caves, performed in rituals, and even solidified in bodily forms. The stories were not only for entertainment, they functioned as essential tools for survival, moral education, bounding emotional relationships and protecting the collective memories of the communities. Storytelling made humans make sense of the abyss, the skies, the seasons, the death and the birth and their fears. As Walter Ong emphasized in his work Orality and Literacy, 1982, oral cultures were shaped by narrative structures in which memory, knowledge and identity were stored and transmitted through repetition, metaphor and myth (Ong, 33&137)18. Even in today's digitalized world shaped extensively by algorithms, stories are still at the very center of the human experience.

Walter Fisher also encapsulated the enduring centrality of storytelling in his concept of homo narrans, which suggests that humans are essentially narrative beings. As he argues, human beings are not just rational actors (homo sapiens), but also they're creatures in need of expressing themselves, connecting with others and making a meaning out of this world through narrative logic and stories. They evaluate the coherence of the narratives not only internal consistency (narrative probability) but also through how well they resonate with personal and cultural truths (narrative fidelity) (Fisher, 6-11)19. This perspective also demonstrates that storytelling is not just a medium to convey events, but also a moral, emotional and social act through which individuals and societies construct and negotiate their identities continuously.

Jerome Bruner’s take on storytelling hits at something fundamental: stories aren’t just ways we describe things—they shape how we see the world, how we figure out who we are, and how we make sense of everything happening around us. In Acts of Meaning (1990), Bruner doesn’t just treat narratives as tools. He sees them as the basic structure for organizing experience and building reality (Bruner, 35)1. Through stories, we explain our choices, find meaning in random events, and try to understand what others feel or intend. Bruner also draws a sharp line between the narrative mode and the logico-scientific mode of thought. The narrative mode, he insists, matters most in childhood and in the way we make sense of daily life (Bruner, 35 & 77-80)1.

For children, stories aren’t just old bedtime lessons or gentle warnings—they’re wild, emotional playgrounds. When kids listen, watch, or even make up their own tales, they’re not sitting there passively. They’re actively experimenting: What does it feel like to be scared? To want justice? To imagine something different? With stories children can experiment new selves, dream up wild futures, worry, hope, and ponder over the world. Whether the story comes from a parent, a book or a movie, they serve both as mirrors and maps. They show where the children are emotionally at and help them to figure out where to go next.

Across generations, stories changed shape. They became more structured and became what we know as folk tales, carrying the collective imagination of the communities. Folk tales packed in warnings, wisdom, and entire worldviews, all wrapped up in memorable images, archetypes, and patterns that managed to travel through time and across borders. Jack Zipes (2006) points out that folk tales aren’t just whimsical or bizarre—they’re loaded with ideology, often carrying the moral codes and class structures of their original societies2. Marina Warner (1994) makes a similar point: fairy tales have always doubled as political and social commentary, sneaking in rebellion or longing under the surface of innocent plots (Warner, XXIII)20.

But folk tales aren’t just dusty relics. They’re living cultural memory. They hold on to not only what happened, but also what could have happened, what people feared and what they hoped for. As Ruth Bottigheimer argues, fairy tales, for example, reveal how societies have historically constructed notions of gender, virtue, power, and danger, and they are never fixed narratives but constantly reshaped by their tellers, audiences, and socio-historical conditions (Bottigheimer 2009, 63–92; 147–176).21. Folk tales shift with every new teller, audience, and era. That flexibility is their secret to surviving for so long. Now, the landscape is totally different. Kids aren’t just huddled around fires or curled up listening to stories passed down by word of mouth. They live in a world overflowing with media—movies, games, YouTube, streaming shows and even stories spun out by AI. Oral storytelling still exists, but the whole ecosystem has exploded. More often than not, it’s screens, not people, doing the telling. And it’s platforms, not communities, deciding what gets told.

This creates a challenge for researchers. When a child is asked about a favorite story, the answer no longer necessarily points to a classical folk tale. It may just as easily be Shrek, Frozen, a YouTube animation, or a TikTok remix. That doesn’t mean the magic of symbolic storytelling has vanished—it’s just evolved. Cristina Bacchilega (2013)22 conceptualizes this transformation through what she calls the “fairy-tale web”: a vast, shifting, and deeply unequal global network in which fairy tales circulate across media platforms, national borders, and power structures. In this web, fairy tales are no longer fixed cultural artifacts but “shape-shifters” that constantly mutate across films, animations, games, social media, and digital markets while retaining recognizable symbolic cores.

For Bacchilega, this expansion of fairy tales into digital and global realm is not culturally neutral. The fairy-tale web is embedded in the structures of global capitalism, where stories circulate not only as cultural heritage but also as commodities, franchises, and branded universes. Familiar motifs such as transformation, romance, rescue, and triumph are endlessly recycled, often reinforcing dominant fantasies of success and happiness. At the same time, these motifs can also be distorted, politicized, and subverted through parody, feminist rewritings, postcolonial retellings, or grassroots remix cultures. Fairy tales thus become what Bacchilega calls “ideologically variable desire machines”: narrative systems that organize what people long for, fear, and imagine as possible under specific historical and economic conditions.

This perspective is especially crucial for understanding children’s relationships with stories today. Children no longer encounter narratives only through oral tradition or printed books, but through algorithm-driven platforms, global entertainment industries, and peer-based digital cultures. They are not just receivers of stories, but active remixers and distributors of meaning. In this sense, storytelling no longer travels only vertically across generations, but also horizontally across platforms, screens, and social networks. A child in Turkey may engage with Kırmızı Başlıklı Kız (Little Red Riding Hood), but just as easily with Disney versions, memes, or global fantasy narratives shaped by Western media industries. Yet each of these forms continues to carry emotional, ideological, and symbolic weight.

For this study, Bacchilega’s framework makes one thing clear: you cannot grasp which stories matter to children without considering the global circuits of media, power, and inequality that shape them. Children in Turkey today grow up at the intersection of folk memory, global entertainment, digital platforms, and deep material divides. Whether a story comes as a folk tale, an animated film, or a viral video, it is always a space where desire, fear, class, and emotion are worked through. Rather than making storytelling less significant, these changes make it even more urgent—as a tool for children to imagine new futures, explore identities, and confront the realities of inequality.

Narratives, therefore, do not function merely as cultural texts in childhood, but as classed emotional resources through which children negotiate fear, hope, justice, and possibility in relation to their lived socio-economic conditions.

2.5 Conclusion

This paper set out to explore how children from different socio-economic backgrounds engage with stories, and how their connections to narratives are shaped by class structures, emotional patterns, and their cultural exposure. I drew on a range of disciplines—psychology, sociology, education, and cultural studies—to create a framework where children’s meaning-making isn’t seen as an innate instinct, but as something constructed by society and symbols.

My intention was to support this framework with direct interviews with children. However, due to the limitations on time and logistics the fieldwork didn’t materialize. Instead, the paper evolved into a deeper theoretical analysis, allowing me to engage more critically with thinkers like Jerome Bruner, Annette Lareau, Jack Zipes, and Walter Fisher. I wanted to highlight that how the childhood is a classed category and how children´s value systems relate with different narratives. The aim was to bring developmental theory into conversation with cultural sociology.

What emerged in the analysis is that storytelling—whether in the form of traditional oral tales, media content, or cultural hybrids— creates a major impact on how children come to imagine morality, envision futures, and make sense of the world. However, access to stories, symbols, and the skills of making meaning from narratives are unequally distributed. Class matters. The heroes they look up to, the futures imagined, and values adopted depend on the material and symbolic landscapes of their lives.

With that said, this paper is just an introduction. I next hope to interview children from varied class backgrounds in Turkey to test and refine ideas developed there. Through fieldwork, I will be able to see directly how stories help children meaning-make: which values shine through, which characters become role models, and exactly how each child, in his or her own way, connects emotionally and symbolically with the narratives around him or her.

Ultimately, I don't think storytelling simply reflects society but rather provides kids with a place to rehearse how to be-who they want to become. Determining how that symbolic rehearsal varies across class lines is not only a sociological question, but an ethical one.
